SAFE vs Convertible Note: What Founders Need to Know Before Raising

September 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Before a startup's first priced equity round, most early capital is raised using one of two instruments: SAFEs or convertible notes. Both allow founders to raise money quickly without the time and expense of negotiating a full priced round, but they work differently in ways that matter for both founders and investors.

This guide explains both instruments in plain terms and covers the practical differences founders should understand before choosing between them.

What Is a Convertible Note?

A convertible note is technically a loan — legally structured as debt — that converts into equity at a future date, typically triggered by the company's next priced funding round. Because it's debt, it includes an interest rate (though this is rarely paid in cash and instead adds to the amount that eventually converts) and a maturity date, after which the note technically becomes due if it hasn't already converted.

Convertible notes were the dominant early-stage instrument before SAFEs were introduced, and remain common in certain markets and specific fundraising situations.

What Is a SAFE?

A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) was introduced by Y Combinator in 2013 specifically to simplify early-stage fundraising. Unlike a convertible note, a SAFE is not debt — it has no interest rate and no maturity date. It represents an agreement that the investor will receive equity at a future triggering event, typically the next priced round, based on terms set in the agreement.

Because SAFEs eliminate the debt characteristics of convertible notes, they're generally simpler and faster to execute, requiring less legal negotiation, which has driven their widespread adoption for early-stage rounds.

Key Terms Common to Both Instruments

Valuation Cap

The valuation cap sets the maximum valuation at which the investment converts into equity, protecting early investors from excessive dilution if the company's valuation increases significantly by the time of the triggering event. A lower valuation cap is more favorable to the investor, since it guarantees a more favorable conversion price.

Discount Rate

The discount rate gives early investors a percentage discount on the price per share compared to investors in the triggering round, compensating them for the additional risk of investing earlier. Common discount rates range from ten to twenty-five percent.

Most Favored Nation Clause

This clause ensures that if the company later offers better terms to another investor before the note or SAFE converts, the earlier investor's terms automatically update to match, protecting against being disadvantaged by more favorable terms offered later.

Why SAFEs Became the Default for Many Startups

SAFEs have become the more commonly used instrument for early-stage U.S. fundraising largely because they eliminate the practical complications of a maturity date. A convertible note technically becomes due for repayment if it reaches maturity without converting, which can create awkward, sometimes contentious situations if a company hasn't yet raised a priced round by that date. SAFEs remove this pressure entirely, simplifying the relationship between founders and early investors.

When a Convertible Note Might Still Make Sense

Despite SAFEs' popularity, convertible notes remain relevant in certain situations — some investors, particularly outside the most SAFE-standardized markets, prefer the more established legal framework and creditor status that debt provides. Additionally, some jurisdictions have less established legal precedent for SAFEs specifically, making convertible notes a more familiar and predictable choice for investors in those markets.

Practical Advice for Founders

Regardless of which instrument you use, a few practices consistently help founders navigate this process well:

  • Use standardized templates (Y Combinator's SAFE template is widely used and recognized) rather than heavily customized agreements, which reduces negotiation friction and legal costs
  • Understand your fully diluted cap table implications before stacking multiple SAFEs or notes with different caps, since this can create complexity that's easy to lose track of across multiple raises
  • Work with a startup-experienced lawyer even when using standardized templates, since jurisdiction-specific and situation-specific nuances still matter

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a SAFE and a convertible note?

A convertible note is technically a debt instrument that accrues interest and has a maturity date, while a SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is not debt at all and has no interest rate or maturity date, converting to equity only upon a future triggering event like a priced funding round.

Which is more common today, SAFEs or convertible notes?

SAFEs have become the more common instrument for early-stage U.S. startup fundraising since their introduction by Y Combinator, largely due to their simplicity and lower legal costs, though convertible notes remain common in certain markets and specific situations.

Do SAFEs and convertible notes give investors ownership immediately?

No, neither instrument grants immediate equity ownership. Both convert into equity at a later triggering event, typically the company's next priced funding round, based on the valuation cap and discount terms specified in the agreement.

Final Thoughts

Both SAFEs and convertible notes serve the same core purpose — allowing startups to raise early capital efficiently without the cost and complexity of a full priced round — but understanding their structural differences helps founders negotiate terms confidently and choose the instrument best suited to their specific investors and market. For most current U.S. early-stage fundraises, SAFEs have become the practical default, though understanding both remains valuable for any founder navigating early fundraising.
