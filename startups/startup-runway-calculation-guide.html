Startup Runway: How to Calculate It and Extend It Before Your Next Raise

September 12, 2026 · 7 min read

Running out of cash remains one of the most common reasons startups shut down — not because the business necessarily failed, but because founders miscalculated or mismanaged their runway, leaving too little time to raise the next round or reach sustainability before the money ran out.

This guide covers how to calculate runway accurately, how much you should maintain at any given time, and practical ways to extend it when needed.

How to Calculate Runway Correctly

Runway is calculated with a simple formula:

Runway (months) = Current Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Net Burn Rate

Monthly net burn rate is your monthly expenses minus your monthly revenue. For a pre-revenue startup, net burn equals gross burn (total expenses), since there's no revenue to offset spending.

Example: A startup with $600,000 in the bank, spending $50,000 per month and generating $10,000 in monthly revenue, has a net burn of $40,000 per month, giving fifteen months of runway ($600,000 ÷ $40,000).

Common Calculation Mistakes

Using gross burn instead of net burn. Ignoring revenue when calculating runway understates how much time you actually have, particularly problematic for startups with meaningful and growing revenue.

Using an average burn rate that ignores trends. If burn rate is increasing month over month due to hiring or growing expenses, using a historical average rather than current or projected burn overstates remaining runway.

Not accounting for known upcoming expenses. Large one-time costs (annual software renewals, planned hires, equipment purchases) should be factored into runway projections rather than only using recurring monthly averages.

How Much Runway Should You Maintain?

Most experienced founders and investors recommend maintaining a minimum of twelve to eighteen months of runway at all times, for a specific practical reason: fundraising typically takes three to six months from first investor conversations to funds actually landing in the bank, even in favorable conditions.

Starting a fundraise with less than six months of runway remaining puts founders in a materially weaker negotiating position, since investors can sense urgency and may use it to extract less favorable terms, or the company may run out of options entirely if the raise takes longer than expected.

Practical Ways to Extend Runway

Reduce Non-Essential Spending

Reviewing all recurring expenses — software subscriptions, contractor engagements, and discretionary spending — for a startup burning cash faster than planned often surfaces meaningful, low-impact savings that don't affect core product development or customer-facing operations.

Delay Non-Critical Hiring

Hiring is typically the largest driver of increased burn rate. Delaying hires that aren't immediately critical to reaching your next milestone, even by a few months, can meaningfully extend runway without slowing core progress.

Accelerate Revenue Where Possible

For startups with any existing revenue, focusing near-term effort on accelerating collections, reducing churn, or closing pipeline deals already in progress can improve net burn meaningfully faster than cutting costs alone, particularly if there's revenue currently sitting in a slow-moving sales pipeline.

Consider a Bridge Round

If runway is tightening but the company isn't yet ready for a full priced round, a smaller bridge round — often structured as a SAFE or convertible note from existing investors — can extend runway to reach a stronger position for the next full raise, without the time and dilution cost of a complete fundraising process.

Renegotiate Existing Contracts

Vendor contracts, office leases, and service agreements are often more negotiable than founders assume, particularly for early-stage companies that vendors want to retain as long-term customers. A direct conversation requesting better terms or payment flexibility sometimes yields meaningful savings with minimal effort.

Tracking Runway Over Time — Not Just Once

Runway isn't a number to calculate once and forget — it should be tracked monthly as part of standard financial review, since burn rate typically changes as the company hires, as revenue grows or contracts, and as spending patterns shift. Many founders build a simple running model updated monthly, projecting runway forward under both current-trend and worst-case burn scenarios.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate startup runway?

Startup runway is calculated by dividing current cash balance by monthly net burn rate (monthly expenses minus monthly revenue), giving the number of months the company can operate before running out of cash at the current spending rate.

How many months of runway should a startup have?

Most experienced founders and investors recommend maintaining at least twelve to eighteen months of runway at all times, since fundraising typically takes three to six months to complete, and starting that process with less than six months of remaining runway creates significant negotiating disadvantage.

What is the difference between gross burn and net burn?

Gross burn refers to total monthly expenses without accounting for revenue, while net burn subtracts monthly revenue from expenses, giving the actual monthly cash decrease. Net burn is the more relevant figure for calculating true runway for companies with any revenue.

Final Thoughts

Runway management is ultimately about preserving optionality — the ability to fundraise from a position of strength rather than desperation, and the time to make thoughtful rather than panicked decisions when circumstances change. Founders who track runway consistently and act early when trends look concerning consistently navigate fundraising and growth decisions from a stronger position than those who only calculate runway when a crisis is already underway.
